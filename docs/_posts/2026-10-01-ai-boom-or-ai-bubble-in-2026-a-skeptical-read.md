**My verdict:** There are two different things happening in 2026, and lumping them together is how people get confused. The *usage* of AI tools is not a bubble — ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are household names, and AI note-takers and coding assistants are now default workplace equipment. The *financing* of the infrastructure behind them looks a lot more like a bubble: spending is being funded increasingly by debt and stock sales, on commitments that stretch out years. I'm leading with that split instead of easing in because the "is it a bubble?" question is usually asked as one question, and answering it as one question gets you the wrong answer for your own situation.

If you're a regular person wondering whether to keep paying $20/month for an AI subscription, this is not your problem. If you hold a lot of concentrated tech stock, or your job depends on an AI startup's next funding round, it is.

## What's Actually Going On

![sec.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-boom-or-ai-bubble-in-2026-a-skeptical-read/2.png)
*Screenshot of sec.gov ([source](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001652044/000119312526251733/d160205dfwp.htm))*

The clearest public evidence isn't in op-eds. It's in company filings, where the numbers are legally binding.

Take Alphabet, because its disclosures are unusually specific:

1. On its Q4 2025 earnings call, management guided 2026 capital expenditures to 
a range of $175 billion to $185 billion
, after 
investing $91 billion in capex in 2025
.
2. By the Q1 2026 call, that guidance had moved up. Alphabet's own securities filing states 
2026 capex is expected to be $180–$190 billion, and that 2027 capex is expected to increase significantly compared with 2026
.
3. In the second quarter of 2026 alone, 
capital expenditures were $44.9 billion
 while 
operating cash flow was $39.1 billion
.

Read step 3 again. For that quarter, the spending was larger than the cash the business generated. That gap has to be filled from somewhere, and the filings say where: 
in June 2026 Alphabet issued stock and mandatory convertible preferred shares for net proceeds of $49.6 billion for general corporate purposes including AI infrastructure capex, and set up an agreement to sell up to $40.0 billion more stock through an at-the-market program
. The same offering documents note 
that over the prior year Alphabet raised more than $85 billion of debt, bringing total debt above $100 billion
, and that 
Berkshire Hathaway agreed to buy $10 billion of stock in a private placement
.

The chip-and-model side is structured similarly. OpenAI and NVIDIA announced a partnership to deploy 
at least 10 gigawatts of AI data centers, with NVIDIA intending to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI progressively as each gigawatt is deployed
.

So: the chip vendor invests in the customer, who buys chips from the vendor. That's not fraud. It is circular, and it's worth naming.

## Where It Breaks

![abc.xyz official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-boom-or-ai-bubble-in-2026-a-skeptical-read/3.png)
*Screenshot of abc.xyz ([source](https://abc.xyz/investor/events/event-details/2026/2025-Q4-Earnings-Call-2026-Dr_C033hS6/default.aspx))*

The bubble case is real, but it's weaker than the loudest version you'll see on social media. Here's where both sides overreach.

- **"Huge capex = bubble" skips a step.** Alphabet is funding this partly from 
$174 billion of operating cash flow over the 12 months ended March 31, 2026
. A company printing that much cash spending heavily is different from a 1999 startup with no revenue.
- **"People use it, so it's fine" skips a different step.** Your $20 subscription does not pay for a gigawatt. Usage being genuine says nothing about whether *this much* capacity, on *this* schedule, earns its cost of capital.
- **Announced capacity isn't delivered capacity.** Gigawatt announcements are press releases; power contracts, land, and transformers are not. Deployments are staged — NVIDIA's first gigawatt with OpenAI was 
slated for the second half of 2026 on the Vera Rubin platform
. Multi-year schedules can slip.
- **Debt changes the failure mode.** Equity-funded overbuilding gets written off quietly. Debt-funded overbuilding forces decisions on a lender's timetable.
- **I could not verify the aggregate.** Plenty of outlets publish a combined "Big Five 2026 capex" total in the $600–725 billion range. I found those figures only in secondary summaries and analyst decks, not in a single primary disclosure I could stand behind, so I'm not quoting a total here.

## One honest caution

Nothing above tells you whether any stock is overpriced — company filings describe spending and funding, not whether that spending will pay off. If your retirement savings are heavily concentrated in a handful of AI-exposed names, that's a conversation for someone licensed to have it.

## What I Learned While Writing This (and What I Think)

Three things genuinely surprised me while reading the filings.

First, how fast guidance moved. Going from 
$175–185 billion
 to 
$180–190 billion
 inside a few months, with 2027 flagged to rise significantly again, tells you the plans are being rewritten mid-flight. Second, the funding mix. I expected capex this big to come out of cash; seeing 
a $49.6 billion equity-and-convertible raise
 explicitly earmarked for AI compute reframed the story for me. Third, the Berkshire placement. That's a famously spending-averse investor on the buy side of an AI-infrastructure raise, which cuts against the simple bubble narrative.

My take: this is a capital-structure bubble sitting on top of a real product boom, and those two things can unwind separately. I think the most likely 2027–2028 outcome isn't ChatGPT disappearing — it's a brutal squeeze on the layer in the middle. Think GPU-rental companies, thin AI wrapper startups, and tools whose only feature is an API call with a logo on it. The models survive. Some of the companies reselling them don't.

What that means for you, concretely: keep the AI tools that save you measurable time, and avoid prepaying for annual plans from small AI startups that don't yet have a profitable business. I'd pay monthly for anything from a vendor I couldn't name two years ago. Some teams will tell you annual billing is the obvious move because the discount is 20%, and for a company like Google or Microsoft I'd agree — the 20% is free money when the vendor isn't going anywhere.

Limits of this piece: I read filings and official announcements. I didn't interview anyone, I don't have inside visibility into data-center utilization, and I deliberately left out the aggregate industry capex number because I couldn't source it primarily. If the bull case is right, I'll have been too cautious about the middle layer, and I'd rather be wrong that direction than tell you to prepay three years of a startup's roadmap.
