"Sydney Sweeney sports ad" climbed US Google Search again on the evening of Tuesday, October 27, 2026 — roughly seven weeks after the ad it refers to first ran. The spike isn't about a new commercial. It's about what happened to one of the athletes who criticized the old one, and about a prominent golfer who took the opposite side.

## What's Happening

The ad in question is for Novig, a US sports-prediction platform. According to Northeastern Global News, Novig's first national campaign — featuring Sweeney nude with sports equipment positioned over her — launched Sept. 9. The tagline: "Just Sports."

CNN reported that the photos and videos released that Wednesday showed Sweeney naked or scantily clad with footballs, a tennis racket and a baseball bat, including one shot lying on a basketball. CNN also reported the Instagram reel version drew 37 million views in four days.

Athletes objected almost immediately. Australian surfer Felicity Palmateer told CNN the campaign was "very damaging" for female athletes and young girls getting into sport. Deadline reported that British sprinter Amy Hunt used a BBC interview after the World Athletics Ultimate Championship to say, "Sydney Sweeney, this is what women in sport look like." Fox News reported that gymnast Gracie Kramer said the ad marketed women's sports through the male gaze.

Novig did not retreat. Fox News reported that on Oct. 11 Sweeney appeared in a second spot wearing only white Novig underwear, covering herself with footballs. Per that campaign's framing, the new ad takes aim at competing prediction platforms such as Kalshi and Polymarket — with lines about insider trading on dictators dying, bots, and someone "betting their 401K on a war," before landing on "Novig is just sports."

The current spike traces to late October. CCN reported that Olympic swimming champion Ariarne Titmus revisited her objections on the Something To Talk About podcast in an episode published Oct. 24, saying, "I probably got double the amount of hate." In an interview published Oct. 22, British golfer Charley Hull defended Sweeney as doing her job and questioned whether athletes condemning the campaign applied the same standard to their own revealing photos.

## Why People Are Searching For It

Three audiences are converging on the same term.

People who saw the second ad on TV or Instagram and want to know what the fuss was. People following the women's-sports argument, who came in through Titmus and Hull. And people reacting to local-newspaper letters pages — one of the news items Google attached to this trend is a St. Louis Post-Dispatch letter calling the underwear spot pornographic, which is the signal that the story has left entertainment media and landed in general-audience complaint territory.

The search term itself is telling. Not "Novig ad," not "Sweeney Novig." People remember it as a *sports* ad, which is precisely the framing the critics object to.

## The Context Most Coverage Skips

Two things are being argued about at once, and they keep getting collapsed into one.

The first is the sexualized-marketing complaint. That one is old and well-documented, and it's the frame most coverage uses.

The second is barely discussed: Novig is a small company buying attention it could never afford to buy conventionally. Tribuna reported, citing Novig co-founder and CEO Jacob Fortinsky, that trading volume roughly tripled since the Sept. 9 launch, with first-time depositors up 361.8% and app downloads up 260.9%. Those are company-supplied figures with no independent audit, and they should be read that way. But the direction is consistent with the one externally observable number — 37 million views in four days on a single reel.

The structure matters too. IBTimes reported that Novig announced Sweeney as a strategic partner and equity holder when the partnership launched Sept. 9. She is not a hired face collecting a fee and leaving. That changes how you read the follow-up ad.

There is also a precedent that almost every write-up gestures at without working through. Sweeney's American Eagle "great jeans" campaign launched July 23, 2025, and drew backlash immediately. That one escalated to the White House communications office, Vice President JD Vance, and Trump himself. The pattern repeated: provocation, outrage cycle, enormous reach, no apology.

And the counter-marketing was fast. Fox News reported that Powerade placed a "Never Just Sports" ad directly beside the Novig campaign in New York — Novig had a Times Square billboard up by Sept. 15. A rival brand treating your controversy as free positioning is its own measure of how big the thing got.

## What We Know and What We Don't

Confirmed: the Sept. 9 launch date, Sweeney's equity stake, the Oct. 11 second ad, the specific athlete criticisms, the Powerade response, and the Titmus and Hull comments from Oct. 22–24.

Not confirmed: Novig's growth figures, which come from the CEO and have not been independently verified. Also unverified is the scale and makeup of the hostility Titmus described — "double the amount of hate" is her own estimate of her inbox, not a measured figure.

Open: whether a third ad is coming, and whether any regulator or platform has acted on the complaints. Nothing in the reporting reviewed here indicates formal action. I found no evidence that any US broadcaster pulled the spot.

## My Take

My read: the interesting story here isn't the nudity. It's that a sports-betting startup figured out it could substitute controversy for a media budget, and the numbers it's citing suggest it worked.

I think the athletes making the sexualization argument are making a real point that keeps getting flattened into a culture-war shouting match. Titmus saying she got more hate than support after criticizing an ad is the part worth sitting with — the cost of objecting landed on her, not on the company that profited. That asymmetry is the actual mechanism, and it's why the second ad exists.

The fair point on the other side: Hull isn't wrong that Sweeney took a job in advertising, and Sweeney has an ownership stake rather than a one-off check, which makes "she was exploited" a harder case to argue. I don't think the critics have fully reckoned with that.

What I'd watch is whether other small betting and prediction platforms copy the playbook. Novig's pitch — we're "just sports," unlike the people letting you wager on dictators — is a regulatory-positioning argument wrapped in a nude photoshoot, aimed at Kalshi and Polymarket. That combination is going to look very attractive to the next startup with no money and a Super Bowl to crash.

I'm not a lawyer and can't say whether any of this runs into advertising or gambling rules in a given state. But the letters-to-the-editor complaints are a weak lever. If this campaign gets stopped, it'll be because a payment processor, an app store, or a state gaming regulator decides it's a problem — not because people found it tacky.
