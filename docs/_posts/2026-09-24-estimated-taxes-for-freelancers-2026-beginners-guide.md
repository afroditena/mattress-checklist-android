If you're freelancing in 2026 and you expect to owe **$1,000 or more in federal tax** after withholding and refundable credits, the IRS expects you to pay in four installments during the year — not in one lump sum next April. 
You generally need to make estimated payments when you expect to owe at least $1,000 after subtracting withholding and refundable credits, and your withholding and credits will be less than the smaller of 90% of the current year's tax or 100% of the prior year's tax (110% if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000, or $75,000 if married filing separately)
. The simplest way for most beginners to stay penalty-proof: take last year's *total tax* line, pay 100% of it (110% if you're over that AGI line) in four chunks, and settle the difference at filing.

## The 2026 payment dates (and why they're not really quarterly)


For the 2026 tax year, the installment dates are April 15, 2026, June 15, 2026, September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027 — and you don't have to make that final payment if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the entire balance with it.


Notice the gaps: two months between the first and second payment, then four months between the third and fourth. The periods are uneven, which trips up people who set a quarterly calendar reminder and assume the money is due every three months. The penalty is also calculated period by period, so overpaying in September doesn't undo a shortfall from June.

## What you're actually paying: income tax *plus* self-employment tax

This is the number-one shock for first-year freelancers. As a sole proprietor you report profit on Schedule C and then owe self-employment tax on your net earnings on top of regular income tax. 
In 2026, self-employment tax is 15.3% of 92.35% of net earnings: 12.4% for Social Security up to a $184,500 wage base, plus 2.9% for Medicare with no cap.
 
An additional 0.9% Medicare tax applies to self-employment income above $200,000 ($250,000 for joint filers, $125,000 for separate filers).


Two things soften the blow: 
self-employed workers can deduct the employer-equivalent portion as an above-the-line deduction, which reduces adjusted gross income
, and SE tax applies to *net profit*, not gross revenue — so legitimate business deductions (software, mileage, home office, health insurance premiums where eligible) shrink both taxes.

A rough planning shortcut many freelancers use: set aside 25–30% of every payment that hits your account. That's a starting point, not a promise — your real rate depends on your bracket, your state, your deductions, and any W-2 income in the household.

## The cost of getting it wrong

The underpayment penalty isn't a flat fine; it's an interest charge on the shortfall, running from each installment due date until you pay. 
The IRS sets that interest rate quarterly — it was 7% for the first quarter of 2026 and 6% for the second.
 Skipping a $3,000 installment for a few months costs tens of dollars, not hundreds. Annoying, but it shouldn't cause panic — and it's a strong argument for paying *something* late rather than nothing at all.

There's also no penalty at all in one common case: 
if you end up owing less than $1,000 after withholding and refundable credits
.

## Four ways to figure your number

| Method | What you do | Best for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prior-year safe harbor (100%/110%)** | Take last year's total tax, divide by 4 | Anyone with a full prior-year return and rising or unpredictable income | You may still owe a big balance in April if income jumped — just no penalty |
| **90% of current year** | Estimate 2026 income and tax, pay 90% of it | Freelancers whose income is clearly *dropping* this year | Requires guessing accurately; a good year blows past your estimate |
| **Annualized income method** | Recalculate each period based on actual income earned so far | Very lumpy income (one huge Q3, quiet Q1) | More paperwork; usually means filing Form 2210 with your return |
| **Extra W-2 withholding** | You or a spouse increase withholding on a paycheck | Side-hustlers with a day job | Only works if there's a paycheck to withhold from |

One quirk worth knowing: withholding is treated as paid evenly across the year, so bumping up a spouse's W-4 in November can still patch a shortfall from earlier periods. Estimated payments don't get that treatment.


Under IRC §6654(d)(1)(B), you avoid the penalty when withholding plus timely estimated payments equal the lesser of 90% of your current-year tax or 100% of prior-year tax — 110% if prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000 ($75,000 MFS).
 You only need to clear one test.

## Who should use this system — and who can skip it

**Good fit:** full-time freelancers, 1099 contractors, Etsy and Upwork sellers, and gig workers with no withholding anywhere. Also anyone whose freelance profit is large enough that a side job's withholding can't absorb it.

**You can probably skip quarterly payments if:** your freelance profit is small and your day-job withholding already covers it (check that you'll owe under $1,000), or this is your first year with essentially no prior-year tax. 
The prior-year safe harbor requires that your prior-year return covered all 12 months.


**Awkward middle ground:** someone who freelanced part of the year and took a W-2 job mid-year. Running the numbers once in July usually beats guessing.

## Concrete steps to set this up this week

1. **Open a separate tax savings account.** Move 25–30% of every client payment into it the day it lands. If it sits in checking, it gets spent.
2. **Pull last year's Form 1040 and find "total tax."** That single line is your safe-harbor baseline. Divide by four (or by 4 after multiplying by 1.10 if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000).
3. **Subtract any withholding** you or your spouse expect in 2026. Whatever's left is what estimated payments need to cover.
4. **Pay electronically.** 
The IRS offers several ways to pay electronically — see irs.gov/payments.
 IRS Direct Pay pulls straight from a bank account; EFTPS lets you schedule all four in advance, which is the "set it and forget it" move.
5. **Recheck in September.** If your income doubled, raise the last two payments. 
If you overestimated, you can adjust using another Form 1040-ES worksheet.

6. **Don't forget your state.** Most states with an income tax run their own estimated schedule, and the dates don't always match the IRS.

## Alternatives worth considering

- **Withholding instead of estimates.** If you have any W-2 income in the household, adjusting a W-4 is less work than four separate payments.
- **An S-corp election.** Sometimes pitched as an SE-tax saver, but it adds payroll filings and costs. It rarely pencils out at low-to-mid freelance profits, and it's a conversation for a CPA, not a blog post.
- **Retirement accounts.** A SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) contribution reduces income tax on your profit (though not SE tax). Contribution limits change yearly — verify the current figure on irs.gov before you plan around it.

## FAQ

**Do I have to pay quarterly taxes my first year freelancing?**

Only if you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding and refundable credits.
 With no prior-year return covering 12 months, you'll need to estimate the current year instead of leaning on the prior-year safe harbor.

**What happens if I miss a quarterly tax payment?**
You accrue an interest-style penalty on the shortfall from that due date. 
The rate is reset quarterly — 7% for Q1 2026 and 6% for Q2 2026.
 Pay as soon as you can to stop the clock.

**Can I just pay it all in one payment in January?**
You can, but the penalty is figured period by period, so earlier shortfalls still count. 
The January 15, 2027 installment can be skipped entirely if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the full balance then.


**How much should I set aside from each client payment?**
Many freelancers use 25–30% as a working rule, then true it up against their actual safe-harbor number. It's an estimate, not a guarantee — high state taxes or a high bracket can push you above it.

**Where do I actually send the money?**

Through the IRS's electronic payment options at irs.gov/payments
, or 
by mailing a Form 1040-ES voucher with a check payable to "United States Treasury," writing your Social Security number and "2026 Form 1040-ES" on it
.
