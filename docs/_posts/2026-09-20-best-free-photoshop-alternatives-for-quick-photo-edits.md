If you just need to crop a headshot, clean up a screenshot, or open a PSD a client sent you, **Photopea** is the best free Photoshop alternative for most people — it runs in your browser, uses a Photoshop-style layout, and opens layered PSD files without an Adobe account. If you'd rather install real software and never see an ad, **GIMP** is the strongest free desktop option. And for fast, template-driven edits on the web, **Pixlr** is a reasonable third pick.

Below is what each one actually gives you for free, where the limits bite, and how they compare.

## Photopea — Best overall free Photoshop alternative

Photopea is a browser-based editor built to feel like Photoshop. The panel layout, keyboard shortcuts, and tool names are close enough that muscle memory mostly carries over, and you can open a file and start editing without creating an account.

Its biggest practical advantage is file compatibility. 
Photopea can open, edit, and export PSD files with layers intact without forcing an upgrade
, which makes it genuinely useful when someone emails you a layered mockup and you only need to swap one headline.

**The free-tier limits:** the free version is ad-supported — there's a banner area alongside the canvas, and that's the main annoyance. 
Upgrading removes ads, widens the editor to the full screen, raises PeaDrive cloud storage from 500 MB to 5 GB, doubles the length of edit history, and adds AI credits.
 
Premium costs $5 per month.
 That storage number sounds harsh, but it rarely matters in practice, since most people open files from their computer or from a connected Google Drive/Dropbox account rather than storing them in Photopea.

**Best for:** quick edits on any computer, PSD files, and anyone who already knows Photoshop's interface.

## GIMP — Best free desktop editor

GIMP is the long-running open-source image editor for Windows, macOS, and Linux. 
It's cross-platform and free software — you can even change the source code and distribute your changes.
 There's no account, no ads, no upsell, and no cloud dependency, which also makes it the safest pick for sensitive images you'd rather not upload anywhere.

The 3.0 release closed a lot of the gap with paid editors. 
It added non-destructive editing for many commonly used filters, with a real-time on-canvas preview
, plus 
improved PSD export and the ability to select multiple layers at once to move or transform them together
. 
Text styling also improved, with outlines, shadows, and bevels that stay editable
.

**The free-tier limits:** there are none in the pricing sense — the honest limitation is the learning curve. GIMP's tool logic and menu naming differ from Photoshop's, so a "quick" edit can take ten minutes the first few times. It's also a real install, not a tab you pop open on a borrowed laptop.

**Best for:** offline editing, privacy-sensitive work, and anyone who wants one permanent tool instead of a subscription.

## Pixlr — Best for fast, casual edits and social sizes

Pixlr is a browser editor split into two modes: a simplified one for one-click adjustments, filters, and social-media sizing, and an advanced one with layers, masks, and selection tools. If your typical task is "make this photo look better and export it at the right dimensions," it's faster than opening a full Photoshop-style workspace.

**The free-tier limits:** this is where you need to be careful. The free plan is ad-supported, caps how many images you can save or export per day, and includes only a small allowance of AI credits for things like background removal and generative fill. 
Pixlr's free tier does let you edit in the browser and try basic AI features, but it's ad-supported with a limited AI credit pool, and paid plans remove ads and raise those credits.
 Pixlr changes its plan structure fairly often, so check pixlr.com/pricing before you count on any specific number.

**Best for:** quick social graphics and light retouching when you don't want a layers-heavy interface.

## Krita — Best free option if you also draw

Krita is a free, open-source painting program for Windows, macOS, and Linux. It's built for illustration rather than photo correction, so it isn't the right tool for exposure fixes or batch resizing. But it has an excellent brush engine, solid layer and mask support, and it can open PSD files — useful if your "photo editing" is really compositing, texture work, or painting over a photo.

**The free-tier limits:** none. Krita is free to download from krita.org; the paid listing in some app stores is simply a way to support development, not a different product.

**Best for:** digital painting, illustration, and photo-based composites.

## FAQ

**Can any of these open Photoshop PSD files?**
Yes. Photopea handles layered PSDs the most reliably, and 
GIMP 3.0 improved its PSD export
. Krita opens PSDs too. Expect some drift on advanced layer effects and smart objects in any non-Adobe tool.

**Is Photopea safe to use with client files?**
Photopea does the editing in your browser rather than requiring an upload to a server for basic work. Still, if you're handling confidential images under an NDA, a local install like GIMP or Krita removes the question entirely.

**Do I need to pay to remove a background?**
Not necessarily. Photopea and GIMP both have manual selection tools that do the job for free with a bit of effort. What you're usually paying for in these apps is the one-click AI version, which is metered by credits on Photopea's and Pixlr's paid plans.

**Which one should I install if I only edit photos a few times a month?**
Don't install anything. Bookmark Photopea, tolerate the ad bar, and move on.
