Here's my bottom line: AI Overviews didn't kill search traffic in 2026 — they **repriced** it. Informational, "how do I do X" content is now nearly worthless as a traffic asset, while comparison, buying-intent, and experience-based content is worth more per query than it was two years ago. If you're still measuring your site's health by total organic sessions, you're tracking the wrong number and you're about to make some very bad decisions.

The quiet part is that this shift happened without users noticing or complaining. Nobody protested. People just stopped clicking, and a whole category of web content silently lost its reason to exist.

## What's Actually Going On

![pewresearch.org official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-overviews-are-rewiring-search-what-changed-in-2026/2.png)
*Screenshot of pewresearch.org ([source](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/))*

### The scale is bigger than most people realize

This isn't a feature anymore, it's the default interface for the internet. At Google I/O in May 2026, Sundar Pichai said 
AI Overviews had passed 2.5 billion monthly active users, and AI Mode — the conversational search experience — had surpassed 1 billion monthly active users in roughly a year
. 
Google also said AI Mode queries have been more than doubling every quarter since launch.


For context on how fast that moved: 
AI Mode joined the billion-user club faster than any Search surface in Google's history.
 There is no "waiting to see if this catches on" phase left.

### The click data is ugly, but it's ugly selectively

The most behaviorally rigorous study we have is still Pew's, which tracked real browsing rather than keyword-tool estimates. 
Analyzing 900 U.S. adults' browsing data, Pew found 58% of users ran at least one search that triggered an AI summary in March 2025; users clicked a traditional result in just 8% of searches with an AI summary versus 15% without one; clicks on links inside the summaries happened in only 1% of visits; and users were more likely to abandon browsing after a page with a summary (26%) than without (16%).
 
Google disputed Pew's methodology, arguing the analysis period overlapped with unrelated algorithm testing.


Ahrefs, using a different method entirely, landed in the same direction. 
Their comparison of forecasted vs. actual CTR suggested AI Overviews reduce position-1 click-through rate by about 34.5%
, and notably, 
99.2% of keywords that trigger AI Overviews are informational in intent
.

That last stat is the whole story, and almost nobody leads with it.

### The rebound is real — and it's a redistribution, not a recovery

Seer Interactive's longitudinal study is the most useful 2026 dataset I've seen. 
It covers 53 brands, 5.47 million queries, and 2.43 billion impressions from January 2025 to February 2026.
 
CTR on AI Overview queries bottomed at 1.3% in December 2025 and climbed back to 2.4% by February 2026 — an 85% jump in two months.


But look at what happened on the other side of the ledger: 
CTR on searches *without* AI Overviews rose from 2.8% in early 2025 to 3.8% by February 2026. Seer's read is that AI Overviews absorb the quick answers, so the users who still click are the ones who want more.
 
Cited pages outperform uncited pages on the same results page, but both still trail no-AIO searches.


And Seer's query-type breakdown contains the one finding that should reset a lot of assumptions: 
at a 95.4% AIO trigger rate, nearly every "X vs Y" informational query in their study had an AI Overview — meaning comparison-page traffic is almost certainly AIO-affected.


## My Take

![seerinteractive.com official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-overviews-are-rewiring-search-what-changed-in-2026/3.png)
*Screenshot of seerinteractive.com ([source](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-2026-update))*

The dominant narrative — "AI Overviews are destroying the open web" — is emotionally satisfying and strategically useless. The better framing is that Google finally automated the part of the web that was always low-value: the 900-word article that exists solely to restate a fact you could have gotten in one sentence.

I don't think that's a tragedy. I think it was overdue. The "how to convert PDF to Word" genre was never content; it was a toll booth built on top of an answer. Google removed the toll booth. Of course traffic fell.

What most takes get wrong is the assumption that the declines are uniform. They aren't. 
Pew found just 8% of one- or two-word searches produced an AI summary, rising to 53% for searches of 10 words or more
 — the long, conversational, explain-this-to-me query is AI Overview territory. The short, brand-specific, "I already know what I want" query largely isn't. Those are different businesses with different futures, and averaging them into one "organic traffic" line hides that completely.

Here's where I'll get specific, because the data pushes in an uncomfortable direction: **the comparison-content safe harbor is closing.** For most of 2025, the advice was "pivot to comparison and buying-intent content, AI Overviews don't touch it." Seer's 95.4% AIO rate on "X vs Y" queries says that window is basically shut for generic comparisons. What survives isn't the *format*, it's the *input* — original testing, real pricing you actually paid, screenshots, opinions a model can't synthesize from three other summaries. An AI Overview can compress consensus. It can't manufacture a firsthand judgment that doesn't exist on the web yet.

To the skeptical reader who says "this is just SEOs whining about a platform change": partly, yes. But the second-order effect is real. 
Pew's data showed Reddit, Wikipedia, and YouTube among the most-cited sources in AI summaries
 — when synthesis pays and origination doesn't, you get more synthesis and fewer people doing the original work that synthesis depends on. That's a slow problem, not a loud one, which is exactly why it's easy to ignore.

## The Counterpoint

![ahrefs.com official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-overviews-are-rewiring-search-what-changed-in-2026/4.png)
*Screenshot of ahrefs.com ([source](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks/))*

The strongest argument against me is that clicks were always a bad proxy for value, and the CTR rebound proves the panic was overblown. 
Seer explicitly notes that lower CTR doesn't always mean worse results — in some cases clicks stayed flat while impressions grew.
 If AI Overviews strip out the low-intent drive-by visitors who were never going to convert, a site can lose 40% of its sessions and lose almost nothing that mattered. Fair. Some of the loudest traffic-collapse stories are businesses discovering their audience was never really theirs.

I still land where I started, for one reason: 
even *cited* pages get fewer clicks than pages on no-AIO searches
. Being the source Google trusts most is still worse, click-wise, than the AI simply not showing up. That's not a quality filter. That's a structural discount on being on the web at all.

## Bottom Line

If you run a content site, a SaaS blog, or a personal brand that depends on search:

- **Stop reporting total organic sessions.** Split your reporting into AIO-present and AIO-absent queries. They're two different channels now with opposite trend lines.
- **Kill your explainer backlog.** Content whose only job is to state a fact is a depreciating asset in 2026. Redirect that effort into things requiring firsthand input — tests, teardowns, pricing you verified, opinions with a name attached.
- **Treat citation as a real KPI**, but don't pretend it replaces traffic. 
Cited pages earn roughly 2.1% CTR versus about 0.9% for uncited pages on the same results page
 — worth chasing, not worth betting the business on.
- **Build one channel Google can't intermediate.** Email, community, podcast, YouTube. Pick one and actually work it.

If you're just a *searcher*, the practical takeaway is smaller but useful: the AI Overview is reliably fine for settled facts and reliably shallow for anything involving judgment, recency, or price. Learn which of your questions are which.

## FAQ

**Are AI Overviews reducing clicks for every kind of search?**
No. 
Ahrefs found 99.2% of AI Overview–triggering keywords are informational in intent
, and 
Pew found only 8% of one- or two-word searches produced a summary versus 53% of searches with 10+ words
. Short, navigational, and brand queries are far less affected than long conversational ones.

**Is the CTR decline reversing?**
Partially — Seer measured a bottom of 1.3% in December 2025 rising to 2.4% by February 2026.
 But 
CTR on searches without AI Overviews rose to 3.8% over the same period
, so the gap hasn't closed.

**Does getting cited in an AI Overview make up for lost traffic?**
It helps and it isn't enough. 
Pew found clicks on links inside AI summaries occurred in just 1% of visits
. Citation is better than invisibility; it is not a traffic strategy on its own.

**Is AI Mode a bigger threat than AI Overviews?**
Not yet in raw reach — 
AI Overviews sit above 2.5 billion monthly users versus AI Mode's 1 billion
 — but 
AI Mode queries have been more than doubling every quarter
, so the trajectory matters more than the current gap.
