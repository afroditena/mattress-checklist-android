**My verdict:** "AI-powered" on a product page tells you almost nothing in 2026, and I think you should treat it the way you treat "all natural" on a cereal box — a category signal, not a fact. It's still worth paying attention to when a vendor names the model, describes the specific task it does, and publishes limits. It's worth ignoring entirely when it appears as a badge on a pricing page with no verb attached. I'm leading with that because the useful question isn't "is this AI?" anymore — it's "what does it do when it's wrong?"

## What's Actually Going On

![ftc.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-powered-in-2026-what-it-means-what-it-hides/2.png)
*Screenshot of ftc.gov ([source](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2023/02/keep-your-ai-claims-check))*

Three things changed the meaning of the phrase, and only one of them is technical.

The first is regulatory. The FTC has been clear that nothing about AI exempts a company from ordinary advertising law. Back in February 2023 it published business guidance telling companies to keep their AI claims in check, and the part I find most useful as a buyer is this: merely using an AI tool during development isn't the same as the product itself containing AI. The agency also warned that performance claims can be deceptive if they lack scientific support, or if they only hold for certain users or under certain conditions.

Then it started enforcing. In September 2024 the FTC announced Operation AI Comply, a law enforcement sweep against companies using AI hype or selling AI tools usable in deceptive ways. The agency has also required companies to substantiate claims about AI safety, bias, and efficacy, and finalized a rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones. The initiative has continued past the change in administration, with ongoing actions over "AI washing" and deceptive advertising.

The second shift is disclosure rules. The EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations have applied since 2 August 2026, covering the marking and detection of AI-generated content and the labelling of deepfakes and certain AI-generated publications. The European Commission's AI Office facilitated a voluntary Code of Practice to help providers comply, but the underlying Article 50 requirements are legal obligations, not optional. Practically, that means more products will *tell* you an AI is involved — which makes the label even weaker as a differentiator.

The third is plain market saturation. When meeting notes, email drafts, code completion, and search summaries all ship with AI inside, "AI-powered" stops describing a product category. It describes the year.

So here's the decoding I'd actually use when a vendor says it:

| What the page says | What it usually means | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "AI-powered" with no verb | Marketing badge | Powered to do *what*, exactly? |
| "AI-assisted drafting" | A model writes, you approve | Can I see and edit before it sends? |
| "Automatically detects / categorizes" | Could be a model, could be rules from 2014 | What happens on a false positive? |
| "Summaries and action items" | Genuine LLM feature | Does it cite the transcript line? |
| "98% accurate" | A claim that needs backup | Tested on what data, by whom? |

That last row isn't hypothetical. The FTC filed a complaint over Workado's unsubstantiated claim that its AI-detection software was 98% accurate.

## Where It Breaks

![ftc.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-powered-in-2026-what-it-means-what-it-hides/3.png)
*Screenshot of ftc.gov ([source](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ai-accomplishments-1.17.25.pdf))*

- **The label survives even when the feature is removed.** Nothing forces a vendor to update "AI-powered" copy when the underlying feature changes or degrades.
- **Accuracy numbers are usually vendor-run.** A vendor's marketing assertion is not substantiation — if there's no published methodology, treat the number as a sales figure.
- **"AI" can mean the vendor calls an API.** Your data may travel to a third-party model provider. The product page rarely says which one.
- **Enforcement is mostly about fraud, not mediocrity.** The FTC cases involve earnings schemes, fake reviews, and bogus robot lawyers. A boring tool with a mildly oversold AI tab isn't getting a complaint filed against it, so don't expect regulators to protect you from disappointment.
- **Disclosure isn't quality.** An EU-compliant "you're talking to an AI" notice tells you the thing exists. It says nothing about whether it's good.

## A Note on the Legal Side

![digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/ai-powered-in-2026-what-it-means-what-it-hides/4.png)
*Screenshot of digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu ([source](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content))*

I'm not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. If your own company markets something as AI-powered, or you're deploying generative tools that reach EU users, the Article 50 duties and the FTC's substantiation expectations are worth a real compliance review rather than a blog post. I'm writing this as a buyer's decoder ring, not a compliance checklist.

## What I Learned While Writing This (and What I Think)

Three things caught me off guard, and I'll be honest about the limits of what I did here: I read regulatory documents and official guidance. I didn't audit any vendor's product claims hands-on, and I couldn't verify which specific companies are currently cleaning up their marketing language.

What surprised me most was how old the clearest rule is. The "using an AI tool to build it doesn't make it an AI product" line dates to FTC guidance from early 2023, well before the current wave of AI badges. The rule was sitting there the whole time.

The second surprise: the DoNotPay "robot lawyer" matter ended with a figure of $193,000 — small enough that I think a well-funded startup would treat it as a cost of doing business rather than a deterrent. That's my read, not the FTC's, and I'd note the counterargument: the reputational hit and the compliance orders probably sting more than the dollar amount.

Third, I expected EU transparency rules to make marketing language more honest. I now think they'll do the opposite in one narrow way. When every chatbot must announce itself, announcing yourself stops being a trust signal and becomes background noise — and the vendors with genuinely strong features will have to differentiate on something else, which is good.

My actual advice: ignore the adjective, chase the verb. If a vendor can tell you the specific task, show you the output before it's final, and name what happens when the model gets it wrong, the AI is probably real and probably useful. If the page says "AI-powered" and then changes the subject, I'd assume there's a rules engine with a new sticker on it — and I'd make the salesperson prove me wrong on a live call with your own data.
