Five things move your credit score, and two of them do most of the work. 
FICO Scores are calculated from credit report data grouped into five categories: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%).
 So if you're starting out, the highest-leverage moves are simple: never miss a due date, and keep your credit card balances low relative to your limits. Everything else is a rounding error by comparison.

## First, what a credit score actually is

![consumerfinance.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/credit-score-for-beginners-what-actually-affects-it/2.png)
*Screenshot of consumerfinance.gov ([source](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/understand-your-credit-score/))*

A credit score is a number generated by a formula that reads your credit report. 
Companies use a mathematical formula—called a scoring model—to create your credit score from the information in your credit report.
 
That information is reported by your lenders to credit reporting companies, the three biggest being Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.


Two things trip up beginners here:

**You don't have one score.** 
You do not have just "one" credit score — each score depends on the data used to calculate it, and it may differ depending on the scoring model, the source of the data, and even the day it was calculated.
 
Most credit scores range from 300–850.
 The number in your banking app may not match what a lender pulls. That's normal, not an error.

**Your income isn't in the formula.** Your salary, savings balance, and job title aren't on your credit report. A $30,000-a-year worker who pays on time can out-score a $200,000 earner who pays late. What's scored is how you handle borrowed money.

## The five factors, ranked by how much they matter

![consumerfinance.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/credit-score-for-beginners-what-actually-affects-it/3.png)
*Screenshot of consumerfinance.gov ([source](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-score-en-315/))*

| Factor | Weight | What it looks at | How fast you can move it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment history | 35% | Whether you've paid past accounts on time | Slow to repair, instant to damage |
| Amounts owed | 30% | How much of your available credit you're using | Fastest lever — one statement cycle |
| Length of credit history | 15% | Age of oldest, newest, and average accounts | Only time fixes it |
| New credit | 10% | Recent applications and newly opened accounts | Months |
| Credit mix | 10% | Cards vs. installment loans vs. mortgage | Slow; don't force it |

Weights: 
payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%)
. 
Those percentages reflect how important each category is in general, and the importance of the categories may vary from one person to another.
 If you have a thin file — say, one card opened eight months ago — length of history carries more weight for you than it would for someone 20 years in.

### Payment history (35%)


Paying your bills on time, every time has the greatest impact on your score.
 
The first thing any lender wants to know is whether you've paid past credit accounts on time, and this is the most important factor in a FICO Score.


The good news for anyone who's already slipped: 
a few late payments are not an automatic "score-killer," and an overall good credit history can outweigh one or two instances of late credit card payments.
 
If you have missed payments, get current and stay current.
 
Creditors typically report late payments as 30-days late, 60-days late, 90-days late, 120-days late, 150-days late, or charge-off
 — which is why paying a few days after the due date (annoying late fee, yes) is very different from letting it ride a full month.

### Amounts owed / utilization (30%)

This is the one beginners underuse. 
The amount of debt you owe makes up 30% of your FICO Score.
 
In a general sense it refers to how much debt you carry in total, but the amount of debt you have is not as significant to your score as your credit utilization.
 
If you're using a lot of your available credit, this may indicate you're overextended — and banks can interpret that to mean you're at higher risk of defaulting.



Experts advise keeping your use of credit at no more than 30 percent of your total credit limit.
 On a $1,500 limit, that's a $450 reported balance. And note the timing detail most people miss: 
your current account balance isn't necessarily the balance on your credit report — the report reflects what your lender sent the bureau, typically your latest statement balance, so even if you pay in full each month, your report may not show $0.
 Paying down before your statement closes, not just before the due date, is what lowers the reported number.

### The remaining 25%


In general, a longer credit history is positive for your FICO Scores, but it isn't required for a good credit score.
 New credit and credit mix are 10% each. 
Having existing credit accounts can be a plus, but too many credit card accounts may hurt your score, and many scoring systems consider the types of accounts you have.
 Translation: don't open a car loan you don't need just to "diversify."

## Concrete steps for your first 90 days

![consumer.ftc.gov official page screenshot](https://afroditena.github.io/mattress-checklist-android/assets/img/credit-score-for-beginners-what-actually-affects-it/4.png)
*Screenshot of consumer.ftc.gov ([source](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-scores))*

1. **Pull your reports.** 
You should check your credit reports at least once a year to make sure there are no errors that could keep you from getting credit or the best available terms on a loan.
 Get them from AnnualCreditReport.com, the federally authorized source, and dispute anything that isn't yours.
2. **Automate the minimum payment.** 
One way to make sure your payments are on time is to set up automatic payments, or set up electronic reminders.
 Autopay the minimum as a floor, then pay more manually.
3. **Pick a target utilization and hit it every month.** Under 30% of your limit as a starting rule; lower generally helps. Set a calendar alert three days before your statement closes and pay the balance down then.
4. **Stop applying for things.** 
To improve your score, focus on paying your bills on time, paying outstanding balances, and avoiding opening several new accounts at the same time.

5. **Leave your oldest card open.** Closing it shrinks your total available credit (raising utilization) and eventually shortens your history.

## Who this matters most for — and who can chill

**Matters a lot right now:** anyone planning to rent an apartment, finance a car, or apply for a mortgage in the next 1–3 years. 
Your credit score can affect whether you'll qualify for credit cards, auto loans, and mortgages — and how much you'll pay for them, and cellphone companies and auto and home insurers also use credit scores.


**Matters less:** if you're debt-free, renting month-to-month with no move planned, and not borrowing for years, obsessing over 10-point swings is wasted energy. Build the habits, check annually, move on.

**Freelancers, note:** lenders look at more than the score. 
An auto lender considers several factors – including your credit score, your credit history, income, debts, and down payment – when deciding what interest rate to offer you.
 A strong score helps, but documented income still matters for approval.

## Alternatives if you're starting from zero

No credit file means no score at all — different problem from a bad score. Realistic on-ramps: a secured credit card (you put down a deposit that becomes your limit), becoming an authorized user on a family member's well-managed card, or a credit-builder loan from a credit union. 
Some credit unions offer free credit counseling services to their members.


And be skeptical of anyone selling a shortcut. 
Improving your credit takes time, and no company can legally remove accurate, negative information from your credit report.


## FAQ

**How long does it take to build a credit score from scratch?**
Scoring models need some reported history before they'll generate a number, so plan on several months of activity, not weeks. There's no guaranteed timeline — it depends on what your lenders report and when.

**Does checking my own credit score hurt it?**
No. Checking your own reports and scores is not the same as a lender's application inquiry. 
While some consumers are reluctant to shop for credit out of fear that they'll harm their score, that negative impact may be overblown.


**Why is my score different on two different apps?**

Each credit score depends on the data used to calculate it, and it may differ depending on the scoring model, the source of the data, and even the day it was calculated.
 
It's up to each lender to determine which credit score they'll use.


**What counts as a good credit score?**

A "good" credit score is generally considered to be in the 670–739 range
 on the 
300–850 scale most credit scores use
. Cutoffs vary by lender and product.

**Should I pay off my card in full or leave a small balance?**
Pay in full. Carrying a balance costs you interest and does not help your score. 
In some cases, a low credit utilization ratio will have a more positive impact on your FICO Scores than not using any of your available credit at all
 — which is about *using* the card, not about carrying debt.
